Where will the fine particles be tomorrow, down to your street?

Every morning Otso takes the 00 UTC analysis of the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service, the ECMWF weather forecast of the same hour and the fires seen by satellite, computes the transport of the air with FLEXGREEN and forecasts PM2.5 from all its sources (traffic, industry, heating, agriculture, dust, sea salt and fires) over all land, 12 hours to 5 days ahead, as an ensemble of 8 members at 0.4° (about 40 km). A generative model then draws the same forecast at 1 km, conditioned on each member, on the weather and on the transport, and the map puts it on a street map. Otso is the coarse model of the Foehn paper, fine-tuned on the live inputs of 2026; the 1 km model is trained on GHAP V1, a satellite–station PM2.5 record.

The forecast

Search a city, a street or an address, use your position, or tap the map: the value of that square kilometre, its class, and how the days ahead look. Scroll or pinch to zoom down to the streets, drag to pan. Layers: the daily mean of the 8 members at 1 km, the number of members above 15 µg m−3 (the WHO 24-hour guideline), and the 40 km forecast every 12 hours (mean, 90th percentile, share above 25 µg m−3).

    Any city, town or village: 34,000 places above 15,000 inhabitants answer instantly (GeoNames, CC BY 4.0); streets, addresses and the other places come from OpenStreetMap as you type (© OpenStreetMap contributors). You can also tap the map or use your position. The headline value is the square kilometre that contains the place; the 12-hour curve is the 0.4° cell (about 40 km) around it.

      How good is it?

      The 1 km forecast at the monitors

      The 1 km model is trained on GHAP V1 (2017–2021) and checked against air-quality monitors that were never used for anything: neither for training, nor for choosing the model, nor for any adjustment. For 2022 the 1 km fields are drawn from the archived 0.4° forecast; 2023 to 2026 are hindcasts of the live chain on the archived inputs. Root-mean-square error of the daily mean at the monitor's own square kilometre, against the monitor's day, compared with the CAMS 0.4° analysis interpolated to the monitor and with a climatology.

      • The 1 km forecast is closer to the monitors than the 40 km analysis it starts from, by 8 to 14 % every year, and the probabilistic score (CRPS) follows. The monitors are in China (CNEMC), Europe, North America and elsewhere (OpenAQ); 2022 also includes AirKorea, Japan and India.
      • Read with care. GHAP, the record the 1 km model learnt from, is itself fitted to about 9,500 monitors of 2017–2022, so a monitor-pixel comparison is favourable to any model trained on it; the gain is smaller one pixel away. The forecast skill of a given day is not known in advance, and the live 1 km chain has no verification yet (the 0.4° chain has one, below).

      What Otso is, and is not

      What it is

      • The coarse Foehn model of the paper, brought up to date, and drawn at 1 km. Otso 1.2 is Foehn-CAMS, the 0.4° version trained on the CAMS analyses of 2017–2022 in the protocol of Microsoft's Aurora, fine-tuned on the live inputs of January–August 2026 because the CAMS system of 2026 differs from the one it learnt from. A second model, a pointwise flow-matching downscaler trained on GHAP V1 (a 1 km satellite–station PM2.5 record), draws the 1 km fields for each of the 8 members, conditioned on the weather forecast and on the transport computed by FLEXGREEN.
      • Live inputs, every morning. The 00 UTC CAMS analysis (13 species and columns), the 00 UTC ECMWF IFS forecast from the open-data service (winds, temperature, humidity, pressure, rain, to +192 h), and the fires of the previous day from GFAS. FLEXGREEN recomputes, for every land cell and every lead, where its air was.
      • A probabilistic forecast. Eight members per day. At 1 km the map shows their daily mean and the number of members above 15 µg m⁻³ (the WHO 24-hour guideline); at 0.4°, every 12 hours, their mean, their 90th percentile and the share above 25 µg m⁻³; the city table and the NetCDF file also give the 10 % and 90 % quantiles.
      • Where there is no 1 km value. The 1 km model covers the land where GHAP is valid, from 60° S to 84° N. Over Greenland and the other permanent ice, where GHAP is not reliable, the map shows Otso's own 0.4° forecast as it is; a few small land areas outside the model's 1 km mask are interpolated from the 1 km values around them or, with none within 50 km, show the 0.4° forecast. All are drawn with a light hatching, and the value box says which source you are looking at.
      • Reproducible. Each day's inputs, their sources and the exact variables used are logged with the forecast (see the inputs field of index.json). The real-time chain is on the main branch of the Otso repository, the model code in the Foehn repository.

      What it is not, yet

      • Not a street-by-street measurement. One value per square kilometre, from a model: streets, tunnels and chimneys are not resolved. The street map is there to find your way, and the 1 km value is a neighbourhood value.
      • Not the 1 km Foehn of the paper. The paper's kilometre-scale forecasts need a 1 km analysis that exists only for the past; the live 1 km fields come from a downscaler of the 0.4° forecast, trained on GHAP. GHAP is itself fitted to monitors (2017–2022), which the station scores above inherit.
      • Not validated in real time at 1 km. The station scores come from 2022 to 2026 hindcasts; the daily 1 km forecast has no live verification yet. The 0.4° chain is verified every morning against the CAMS analyses (scores).
      • Not an operational service. It runs on a shared supercomputer (LUMI) when a GPU is free; a day can be late or missing. The last seven days of the 0.4° forecast are kept, the latest day of the 1 km tiles.
      • Not an alert. See the notice at the top. Otso is named after the bear of Finnish folklore. Versions: 1.1, the paper's weights on live inputs (10 October 2026); 1.2, fine-tuned on January–August 2026; the 1 km layer since 4 November 2026. A thing of the forest, best observed from a distance.

      The previous page, with the 0.4° forecast drawn on a plain world map without a street basemap, is kept as the classic map; it also works in browsers without WebGL.
